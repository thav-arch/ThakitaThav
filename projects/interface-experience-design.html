01

Coca-Cola · Customer journey intelligence

Understanding the moments behind consumer decisions.

A purchase, told as a path

The same data could be five frequency tables — one per stage. Each would be accurate, and together they’d say almost nothing about a journey. The tree puts the stages side by side in decision order — why people went out, whether it was planned, where they decided, how they got it, what came with it — and one highlighted path carries the reader across.

Five stages with 7, 2, 4, 4 and 3 options make 672 possible paths. Showing them all produces a tangle; showing one in brand red, with every alternative still visible in grey, keeps the whole space on screen without asking for attention.

  • Context bar OU, Occasion, Category and Brand stated above the tree, so scope travels with every screenshot.
  • Two reading modes Tree View for the story, Table View for lookup; Total vs Weekly / Non-weekly shoppers.
  • Bar-in-node each option pairs a value for precision with a bar for instant comparison.

One metric, five perspectives

A second Coca-Cola surface, the Performance Snapshot, looks like a table of four metrics — but each row has a radio button, the selected row is tinted, and every other panel is titled after it: “Total Value Across Key States”, “…Across Channels”, “…Across RTB Brands”. The metric list is the page’s selector, so one choice reframes the whole snapshot.

Combining summary and control removes a separate dropdown and makes the page’s subject impossible to miss. The trade-off is discoverability: a radio is a quiet affordance for such an important control.

02

NAPA · Customer loyalty analytics

Making customer loyalty measurable.

Twelve numbers, four questions

A loyalty programme is healthy only if several things are true at once — acquisition can rise while existing members drift away. The scorecard’s overview groups twelve KPIs into four bands named after the question they answer, three cards each, with an identical card anatomy throughout.

Each value sits beside its change versus prior year: the absolute gives scale, the change gives judgement. Rates move in percentage points, counts in percent — avoiding the “retention is up 4%” ambiguity.

  • CustomersWho is in the programme, and are they staying?
  • SalesWhat are members worth?
  • TransactionsHow do members buy, and do they use rewards?
  • BehaviorHow engaged are members over time?

Who is changing, and when

The Trends view pairs a population lens with a time lens. Eight segment cards cover every High / Low combination of Recency, Frequency and Spend, always in the same binary order — so a segment’s position is part of its definition, and nothing hides behind a persona nickname.

Sales, a sum, is drawn as bars; AOV, an average, as a line — the chart form tells you what kind of number you’re reading. Each chart has a Metric switch, so new questions reuse the same frame instead of adding charts.

Design opportunity — not an implemented feature

Colour should say “good” or “bad”, not “up” or “down”.

Change chips are coloured by direction, which matches meaning for most metrics. But a rising lapse rate, or more days since a member’s last purchase, is unfavourable even though it goes up. A per-metric polarity lets colour encode favourability while an arrow keeps encoding direction.

03

Marketing & campaign analytics

Connecting marketing investment to performance.

Spend is reported in currency; performance in clicks, views, engagements and revenue — across channels and campaigns that each report differently. Channel and Campaign views put money in and money out side by side, then explain the relationship through efficiency ratios, Campaign / Media / Platform filters and a single trend with a metric selector.

Before any layout, the metrics sort into three kinds — and half the efficiency measures improve as they fall. That one fact shapes every comparison: “best in column” has to follow each metric’s polarity, or the table rewards the most expensive channel.

Newell · Project & business intelligence

Bringing operational complexity into focus.

Navigable, not sparse

A research portfolio sliced by region, segment, business unit, method, team, brand, vendor, audience and approach — every quarter, on one page. The design doesn’t reduce the amount of information; it reduces the cost of finding your way through it.

Every breakdown is “Projects by X” with the same share and spend columns. Organisational dimensions sit above operational ones, above audience and approach, so the page reads like an outline. Totals come first; the period filter sits at the top, the segment filter directly above the band it changes.

05

One grammar, three brands.

The dashboards don’t share a palette — they share a way of reading. Brand colour frames identity and selection; a reserved pair carries performance meaning; a separate sequence encodes chart categories.

Coca-Cola

Red is the only accent — active tab, selected metric, highlighted path.

NAPA

Navy for values and structure; gold only on card edges, never on data.

Newell

A cool four-colour series reused in order across donuts and stacked bars.

Gutters shrink as density grows

Every dashboard is a grid of bounded white panels on a light canvas. What changes is how many panels a row holds and how much air sits between them — generous inside NAPA’s twelve-card overview, tight inside Newell’s parallel tables — and that tracks the density of the data.

What I’d push further

White labels on light chart fills (about 1.6:1 on Newell’s sky blue) and brand red as small text (about 4.3:1) fall short of WCAG AA. Both are fixable without touching the brand: dark labels on light fills, red reserved for larger text — alongside polarity-aware colour and definitions on hover.

A dashboard is an argument about what matters first.

The hardest problems were rarely about drawing charts. They were about ordering: which measure a person sees first, which comparison makes a number meaningful, and how far into the detail someone needs to go before they can act — while letting each client’s brand frame the experience without competing with the data.
